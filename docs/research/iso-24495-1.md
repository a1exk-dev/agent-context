## How this research was done, and its limits

I did not read the text of the standard. The ISO OBP preview is the free official preview, but its Terms of Use (section "Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning Uses") forbid using ISO content to let an AI system ingest, restate, or summarise it, unless a separate licence allows it. An AI agent that accepts those terms and then summarises the preview breaks them. So I did not accept the terms and did not open the preview. For the same reason I did not open copies of the standard or its drafts on third-party hosts (iTeh sample PDFs, a Scribd copy of the DIS, the ANSI and Accuris preview PDFs).

Consequence: every statement below about the content of the standard comes from the ISO catalogue page (public abstract and metadata) or from secondary sources. The strongest secondary sources are people who drafted or edited the standard (Schindler, Spivak) and the plain-language bodies that started it (IPLF, Center for Plain Language). Each claim names its source. A human who buys the standard can check the guideline list against clause 4 to 8 and Annex A.

History (Schindler): the IPLF agreed a definition of plain language in 2014 and set up a standard committee in 2017. Standards Australia took the proposal to ISO, and TC 37 approved it in 2019. An 8-member drafting committee wrote the drafts; Clarity, the Center for Plain Language, PLAIN, the International Institute for Information Design, and the European Parliament gave feedback.

## 2. Scope (ISO catalogue abstract, paraphrased)

- The standard sets governing principles and guidelines for developing plain-language documents. The guidelines explain how to interpret and apply the principles.
- It is for anybody who creates or helps create documents. The widest use is documents for the general public, but it also applies to technical writing, legislative drafting, and the use of controlled languages.
- It applies to most written languages. All examples are in English.
- Limits: it covers only printed or digital information that is mainly text (a note says creators of podcasts or videos can still find it useful). It does not repeat accessibility guidance; it points to WCAG and EN 301 549 for that.

## 3. Definition of plain language

The definition is clause 3.1 (Narratology summary, <https://narratology.co.uk/plain-language-iso-24495-narratology/>). Paraphrase: a communication is in plain language when its wording, structure, and design are so clear that the intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information.

This is the definition the IPLF adopted in 2014, which the IPLF publishes freely on its own site (IPLF definitions page; Center for Plain Language; Schindler). A repo can cite the IPLF wording with attribution instead of the ISO clause.

What the definition implies, according to the drafters:

- Plain language is measured by the result for the reader, not by properties of the text (Schindler; Plain Numbers, <https://plainnumbers.org.uk/news-insights/find-it-understand-it-use-it-the-iso-standard-redefines-plain-language-as-a-user-outcome/>).
- The standard says plain language looks at how well readers can use the document, not at mechanical measures such as readability formulas. Spivak, the standard's editor, reports that this is the only mention of readability formulas in the standard, and that the working group rejected them on purpose (Spivak; Schindler quotes the same sentence).

## 4. The four governing principles and their guidelines

Structure of the standard (Schindler): Foreword, Introduction, Scope, Normative references, Terms and definitions, Governing principles, Guidelines (one subclause per principle), Annex A (overview of principles and guidelines), Annex B (sample checklist). Each guideline has detailed guidance and a few examples.

The principle names below are the ones the IPLF publishes. The guideline titles are as Schindler lists them (secondary source; not checked against the standard text). The numbers are this file's, not the standard's clause numbers.

### Principle 1: Readers get what they need (relevant)

1. Identify the readers.
2. Identify the readers' purpose.
3. Identify the context in which readers will read the document.
4. Select the document type or types.
5. Select the content that readers need.

### Principle 2: Readers can easily find what they need (findable)

1. Structure the document for readers.
2. Use information design techniques that help readers find information.
3. Use headings that help readers predict what comes next.
4. Keep supplementary information separate.

### Principle 3: Readers can easily understand what they find (understandable)

1. Choose familiar words.
2. Write clear sentences.
3. Write concise sentences.
4. Write clear and concise paragraphs.
5. Consider images and multimedia.
6. Project a respectful tone.
7. Make sure the document is cohesive.

### Principle 4: Readers can easily use the information (usable)

1. Evaluate the document continually while you develop it.
2. Evaluate the document further with readers.
3. Continue to evaluate how readers use the document after publication.

Practitioner summaries add detail that I could not check against the standard: separate background information from the main content (MT-G, <https://www.mt-g.com/en/news/blog/iso-24495-1>); handle technical terms carefully (Plain Numbers); match style and tone to the reader's situation (MT-G); keep terms consistent (ITLawCo, <https://itlawco.com/iso-24495-12023-plain-language-standard/>). One summary (Hansem) lists different principle names ("actionable", "consistent"); it disagrees with the IPLF and the drafters, so I ignored it.

## 5. Reader, purpose, context, and success

- **Reader.** The standard is written around "intended readers", the people a document is for. Principle 1 starts by identifying them. The public sources do not show whether clause 3 defines "reader" or "intended reader" as a term. Unknown without the text.
- **Purpose.** In the standard, purpose belongs to the reader, not the author: the second Principle 1 guideline is to identify what readers want to do with the document. Context (the third guideline) is where and how they will read it. Readers, purpose, and context then drive the choice of document type and content (the fourth and fifth guidelines).
- **Success.** A document succeeds when the intended readers can easily find, understand, and use what they need (the definition). Principle 4 makes this the test: evaluate during writing, with real readers before publication, and with readers after publication. Readability formulas are not a success measure (Spivak).
- **Evaluation method.** Annex B is a sample checklist (Schindler). Its content is not public.
- **Conformance.** The public sources do not say whether the guidelines use "shall" or "should", or whether a conformance claim is defined. The scope calls them guidelines, and the IPLF describes Part 4 (in development) as the part for organisations that want certification. Unknown without the text.

## 7. Copyright and terms for a public repo

ISO:

- ISO owns the copyright in its publications and in all content on iso.org. Copying any of it, in whole or in part, needs written permission (ISO copyright page). This includes the catalogue abstract, so this file paraphrases it.
- ISO's user guide says that citing standards or including extracts is encouraged when the acknowledgement is correct and the licence conditions are respected, but you must contact ISO for authorisation. It also forbids using parts of a standard in other documents or software without permission, and forbids translating or modifying a standard (PUB100206).
- The OBP Terms of Use (2026-09-18) grant view-only access for personal, internal, and informational use. They forbid copying, publishing, and creating derivative works. They also forbid any use of ISO content to let an AI or machine-learning system ingest, restate, summarise, or generate output from it, unless a separate licence from ISO, an ISO member, or an authorised distributor allows it. ISO opts out of the EU text-and-data-mining exception (Directive (EU) 2019/790, Article 4).
- Permission requests go to the ISO Central Secretariat or the national ISO member (ISO copyright page).

What this means for this repo (my reading, not legal advice):

- Safe: cite the standard by number and title; name the four principles and the reader-outcome idea in our own words; quote the IPLF 2014 definition from the IPLF with attribution; link to the ISO catalogue.
- Not safe without ISO permission: paste clause text, the Annex A table, or the Annex B checklist; publish a close paraphrase of the guidance under each guideline.
- Agent use: the OBP AI clause covers content accessed through the OBP. A purchased copy comes with ISO's customer licence, which I did not read. Before an agent reads a purchased copy to build or check the skill, a human should check that licence for a similar AI clause.

CAN-ASC-3.1:2025: free to read and share for non-commercial use, but its legal notice forbids reproducing portions of it in other public works without written permission from Accessibility Standards Canada. Paraphrase and citation are the safe route here too.
